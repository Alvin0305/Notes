    Software Engineering - Midsem Exam Answers (Part 1)¶

    Based on the textbook "Object-Oriented Software Engineering: Practical Software Development using UML and Java" by Timothy C. Lethbridge and Robert Laganière

    1. SE INTRODUCTION - ANSWERS¶

    Short Answer Questions¶

    Q1. Define Software Engineering. What makes it different from programming?

    Answer: Software Engineering is the process of solving customers' problems by the systematic development and evolution of large, high-quality software systems within cost, time and other constraints.

    Difference from programming: - Programming is just one activity of software engineering (implementation) - Software engineering includes requirements gathering, design, implementation, verification, and maintenance - Software engineering focuses on solving customer problems, not just writing code - Software engineering involves systematic, disciplined approaches rather than ad-hoc coding

    Q2. List and briefly explain the four main activities of software engineering.

    Answer: 1. Requirements Gathering: Understanding and documenting what the customer needs 2. Design: Creating a plan for how the software will be built (architecture, detailed design) 3. Implementation: Writing the actual code based on the design 4. Verification and Validation: Testing to ensure the software meets requirements and works correctly

    Q3. What are the three main types of software systems? Give one example of each.

    Answer: 1. Custom software: Developed specifically for one customer (e.g., a bank's internal trading system) 2. Generic (off-the-shelf) software: Sold to multiple customers (e.g., Microsoft Office) 3. Embedded software: Built into hardware devices (e.g., software in a microwave oven)

    Q4. Explain the difference between a customer and a user in software engineering context.

    Answer: - Customer: The person or organization who pays for the software. They decide what features are needed and approve the budget. - User: The person who actually uses the software to perform their work. They may have different needs than the customer. - Sometimes they are the same person, but often not. Understanding both is crucial for success.

    Q5. What is the 80-20 rule (Pareto Principle) and how does it apply to software engineering?

    Answer: The 80-20 rule states that 80% of the value to the customer is delivered with 20% of the work.

    Application to SE: - Focus on the most important features first - In iterative development, deliver the most valuable 20% of features in early iterations - Helps prioritize requirements and manage scope - Explains why incremental development is effective

    Long Answer Questions¶

    Q6. Discuss the importance of understanding the customer and user in software engineering. What techniques can be used to gather information about their needs?

    Answer: Importance: - Software exists to solve customer problems - without understanding them, we build the wrong thing - Users must be able to work efficiently with the software - Poor understanding leads to project failure, unused software, endless change requests - Ensures both utility (solves the problem) and usability (easy to use)

    Techniques for gathering information: 1. Interviews: One-on-one discussions with stakeholders 2. Brainstorming: Group sessions to generate ideas 3. Use case analysis: Understanding how users will interact with the system 4. Prototyping: Creating mock-ups to get feedback 5. Domain analysis: Studying the business domain and existing systems 6. Observation: Watching users work with current systems 7. Questionnaires: Surveys for large user groups

    Q7. Explain why software engineering is considered a labor-intensive activity. What are the implications of this for project management?

    Answer: Why labor-intensive: - Software is built by people writing code, not by machines - Requires skilled professionals (software engineers) - Much of the work is creative and intellectual - Automation is limited compared to manufacturing

    Implications for project management: - Need for effective team communication and coordination - Importance of training and skill development - Difficulty in measuring progress (can't just count widgets) - Need for good cost estimation techniques - Importance of team structure and organization - Risk of personnel shortfalls - Communication overhead increases with team size

    Q8. Describe the nine themes that are incorporated throughout the textbook for effective software engineering.

    Answer: 1. Understanding the customer and user: Focus on solving their problems 2. Basing development on solid principles and reusable technology: Apply well-understood knowledge 3. Object orientation: Use abstraction, inheritance, polymorphism 4. Visual modeling using UML: Communicate designs effectively 5. Evaluation of alternatives in requirements and design: Make informed decisions 6. Incorporating quantitative and logical thinking: Use metrics and formal methods 7. Iterative and agile development: Deliver in small increments 8. Communicating effectively using documentation: Keep stakeholders informed 9. Risk management in all software engineering activities: Identify and mitigate risks

    2. EVOLUTION OF SOFTWARE ENGINEERING TECHNIQUES - ANSWERS¶

    Short Answer Questions¶

    Q1. What was the "software crisis" that led to the development of software engineering as a discipline?

    Answer: The software crisis occurred in the 1960s-1970s when: - Software projects were consistently over budget and behind schedule - Software quality was poor with many defects - Software often failed to meet user needs - Maintenance was extremely difficult - Projects were becoming larger and more complex

    This led to the recognition that software development needed systematic, engineering-like approaches rather than ad-hoc programming.

    Q2. How has the focus of software engineering evolved from the 1960s to the present day?

    Answer: - 1960s-1970s: Focus on coding, structured programming - 1980s: Focus on design, structured analysis and design - 1990s: Rise of object-orientation, UML, process models - 2000s-present: Agile methods, iterative development, emphasis on people and communication, DevOps

    The evolution moved from code-centric to process-centric to people-centric approaches.

    Q3. What is the difference between structured programming and object-oriented programming?

    Answer: | Aspect | Structured Programming | Object-Oriented Programming | |--------|----------------------|----------------------------| | Focus | Procedures/functions | Objects that contain data and behavior | | Data | Separate from functions | Encapsulated with functions | | Reuse | Limited | High through inheritance | | Modularity | Functions | Classes | | Communication | Function calls | Message passing |

    Q4. Explain the shift from waterfall to iterative/agile approaches in software development.

    Answer: Reasons for shift: - Waterfall assumes requirements are fully understood upfront - rarely true - Changes are expensive in waterfall - Customers want to see progress early - Risk is not addressed until late in waterfall - Iterative approaches allow learning and adaptation

    Benefits of iterative/agile: - Early delivery of working software - Better handling of changing requirements - Reduced risk through early feedback - Customer satisfaction through involvement - Continuous improvement

    Long Answer Questions¶

    Q5. Trace the evolution of software development methodologies from the early days to modern agile approaches. What factors drove these changes?

    Answer: Evolution:

    1. 1950s-1960s: Opportunistic Approach
    2. Ad-hoc coding without systematic process

    3. Problems: Poor quality, unmaintainable code

    4. 1970s: Structured Programming

    5. Focus on clear control structures
    6. Top-down design

    7. Still code-centric

    8. 1980s: Structured Analysis and Design

    9. Data flow diagrams
    10. Entity-relationship diagrams

    11. Separation of analysis and design

    12. 1990s: Object-Oriented Methods

    13. OOP languages (C++, Java)
    14. UML for modeling

    15. Unified Process

    16. 2000s-present: Agile Methods

    17. XP, Scrum, etc.
    18. Lightweight processes
    19. Focus on people over processes

    Driving factors: - Increasing software complexity - Need for faster delivery - Changing requirements - Recognition that people are key - Desire for better quality - Cost pressures

    Q6. Discuss how the introduction of UML changed software engineering practices. What problems did it solve?

    Answer: UML Introduction: - Unified Modeling Language standardized in 1997 - Combined best of multiple OO modeling notations

    Problems it solved: 1. Inconsistent notations: Before UML, many competing notations (Booch, OMT, OOSE) 2. Communication: Provided common visual language for teams 3. Documentation: Standard way to document designs 4. Tool support: Enabled CASE tools 5. Abstraction: Helped think at higher level than code

    Impact: - Became industry standard for OO modeling - Essential for design communication - Foundation for model-driven development

    Q7. Compare and contrast the traditional "opportunistic approach" with modern software engineering practices. What are the problems with the opportunistic approach?

    Answer: Opportunistic Approach: - Start coding immediately without analysis or design - Fix problems as they arise - No systematic process

    Problems: 1. Requirements not understood → wrong system built 2. No design → poor architecture, hard to maintain 3. No testing plan → many defects discovered late 4. No documentation → knowledge lost 5. No project management → over budget, late delivery 6. Poor quality → customer dissatisfaction

    Modern SE Practices: - Systematic requirements gathering - Design before coding - Planned testing - Documentation - Project management - Quality assurance throughout

    3. SOFTWARE DEVELOPMENT LIFE CYCLE (SDLC) - ANSWERS¶

    Short Answer Questions¶

    Q1. List the phases of the Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC) in their typical order.

    Answer: 1. Requirements Gathering and Analysis 2. System Design 3. Implementation (Coding) 4. Testing (Verification and Validation) 5. Deployment 6. Maintenance

    Q2. What is the purpose of the requirements gathering phase in SDLC?

    Answer: - Understand what the customer needs - Document functional and non-functional requirements - Define system scope - Establish acceptance criteria - Create basis for design and testing - Get stakeholder agreement

    Q3. Why is the maintenance phase considered the most expensive phase of the SDLC?

    Answer: - Software typically remains in use for many years - Maintenance includes: - Corrective maintenance (fixing bugs) - Adaptive maintenance (adapting to new environments) - Perfective maintenance (adding new features) - Preventive maintenance (improving quality) - Studies show maintenance can cost 60-80% of total lifecycle cost - Poor initial design makes maintenance more expensive

    Q4. What is verification and validation (V&V) and when is it performed in the SDLC?

    Answer: - Verification: "Are we building the product right?" - checking that the software meets its specification - Validation: "Are we building the right product?" - checking that the software meets user needs

    When performed: - Throughout the SDLC, not just at the end - Requirements review (validation) - Design review (verification) - Code review (verification) - Testing (both verification and validation) - Acceptance testing (validation)

    Long Answer Questions¶

    Q5. Describe the complete Software Development Life Cycle. Explain what activities are performed in each phase and how they relate to each other.

    Answer: 1. Requirements Gathering and Analysis: - Activities: Interview stakeholders, analyze domain, document requirements, create use cases - Output: Software Requirements Specification (SRS)

    2. System Design: - Activities: Architectural design, detailed design, database design, interface design - Output: Design documents, UML diagrams

    3. Implementation: - Activities: Coding, unit testing, code review - Output: Source code, unit test results

    4. Testing: - Activities: Integration testing, system testing, acceptance testing - Output: Test reports, defect reports

    5. Deployment: - Activities: Installation, data migration, user training - Output: Deployed system, documentation

    6. Maintenance: - Activities: Bug fixes, enhancements, adaptations - Output: Updated system

    Relationships: - Each phase builds on the previous - Feedback loops allow going back to earlier phases - Traceability links requirements through to code and tests

    Q6. Explain the concept of traceability in the context of SDLC. Why is it important?

    Answer: Traceability is the ability to follow the life of a requirement from its origin through implementation and testing.

    Types: - Forward traceability: Requirements → Design → Code → Tests - Backward traceability: Tests → Code → Design → Requirements - Bidirectional traceability: Both directions

    Importance: 1. Impact analysis: When requirements change, know what needs to be modified 2. Verification: Ensure all requirements are implemented 3. Testing: Ensure all requirements are tested 4. Maintenance: Understand why code exists 5. Compliance: Meet regulatory requirements 6. Project management: Track progress against requirements

    Q7. Discuss why requirements changes are inevitable in software development. How should a software engineer handle these changes throughout the SDLC?

    Answer: Why changes are inevitable: - Customers learn more as they see the system - Business environment changes - Technology changes - New stakeholders emerge - Initial requirements were incomplete or incorrect - Competitive pressures

    Handling changes: 1. Expect changes: Plan for them from the start 2. Iterative development: Deliver in increments to get early feedback 3. Change control process: - Document change requests - Analyze impact (cost, schedule, quality) - Get approval from stakeholders - Implement approved changes 4. Traceability: Maintain links to assess impact 5. Configuration management: Track all versions 6. Communication: Keep all stakeholders informed 7. Prioritization: Focus on most valuable changes

    4. PROCESS MODELS - ANSWERS¶

    Short Answer Questions¶

    Waterfall Model¶

    Q1. Draw and label the waterfall model. List its phases in order.

    Answer:

    ┌─────────────────┐
│   Requirements  │
└────────┬────────┘
         ▼
┌─────────────────┐
│      Design     │
└────────┬────────┘
         ▼
┌─────────────────┐
│  Implementation │
└────────┬────────┘
         ▼
┌─────────────────┐
│  Verification   │
└────────┬────────┘
         ▼
┌─────────────────┐
│   Maintenance   │
└─────────────────┘

    Phases: Requirements → Design → Implementation → Verification → Maintenance

    Q2. What are the main limitations of the waterfall model?

    Answer: 1. Requirements must be complete before design starts - rarely possible 2. No working software until late in the process 3. High risk - problems discovered late 4. Difficult to accommodate changes 5. No customer feedback during development 6. Assumes phases don't overlap 7. Not suitable for complex or innovative projects

    Q3. When is the waterfall model most appropriate?

    Answer: - Requirements are well-understood and unlikely to change - System is similar to previous systems - Technical challenges are well-understood - Short project with clear deliverables - Regulatory environment requires complete documentation upfront - Example: Porting an existing system to a new platform

    Iterative Waterfall Model¶

    Q4. How does the iterative waterfall model differ from the pure waterfall model?

    Answer: The iterative waterfall model allows feedback loops between phases: - Can go back from design to requirements if issues found - Can go back from implementation to design - Can go back from testing to implementation

    This makes it more flexible than the pure sequential waterfall.

    Q5. What is the advantage of allowing feedback loops between phases in the iterative waterfall model?

    Answer: - Problems discovered in later phases can be fixed by going back - Reduces risk of building the wrong system - Allows for some requirements refinement - More realistic than pure waterfall - Still maintains overall sequential structure

    Prototyping Model¶

    Q6. What is a prototype in software development? What are the two types of prototypes?

    Answer: A prototype is a preliminary version of the system used to explore requirements or design options.

    Two types: 1. Throwaway (disposable) prototype: Built quickly to learn, then discarded 2. Evolutionary prototype: Built and refined into the final system

    Q7. What is the main purpose of prototyping?

    Answer: - Explore requirements when they're not well understood - Get early feedback from users - Reduce risk of building the wrong system - Validate design approaches - Demonstrate feasibility - Help users articulate needs they couldn't express verbally

    Q8. Explain the difference between throwaway prototyping and evolutionary prototyping.

    Answer:

    Aspect Throwaway Evolutionary
    Purpose Learn and discard Build upon
    Quality Quick and dirty Production quality
    Code Discarded Kept and refined
    Risk Lower (no commitment) Higher (foundation for system)
    When used Early requirements Throughout development

    Incremental Model¶

    Q9. What is incremental development? How does it differ from the waterfall model?

    Answer: Incremental development delivers the system in small, usable increments (pieces).

    Differences from waterfall:

    Waterfall Incremental
    Deliver all at once Deliver in parts
    One big release Multiple small releases
    Late feedback Early and continuous feedback
    High risk Lower risk
    Difficult to change Easier to accommodate changes

    Q10. What are the advantages of the incremental model?

    Answer: 1. Early delivery of working software 2. Customer gets value sooner 3. Feedback from early increments improves later ones 4. Lower risk - problems found early 5. Easier to manage changes 6. Better resource utilization 7. If project fails, something is still delivered

    V-Model¶

    Q11. Draw the V-model and explain its structure.

    Answer:

            Requirements
           /\
          /  \
         /    \
    System    System
    Design    Testing
       /\      /\
      /  \    /  \
     /    \  /    \
  Module  Integration
  Design    Testing
     /\      /\
    /  \    /  \
   /    \  /    \
Coding  Unit Testing

    Structure: - Left side (descending): Development activities (requirements → design → coding) - Right side (ascending): Testing activities (unit → integration → system) - Each development phase has a corresponding testing phase

    Q12. What is the relationship between the left side and right side of the V-model?

    Answer: - Each level on the left has a corresponding test level on the right - Requirements ↔ System Testing - System Design ↔ Integration Testing - Module Design ↔ Unit Testing - The V-model emphasizes that testing should be planned from the start - Test plans are created during the corresponding development phase

    Evolutionary/Spiral Model¶

    Q13. Who developed the spiral model? What are its four quadrants?

    Answer: The spiral model was developed by Barry Boehm in 1988.

    Four quadrants: 1. Determine objectives, alternatives, constraints 2. Evaluate alternatives, identify and resolve risks 3. Develop and verify next-level product 4. Plan the next iteration

    Q14. What is risk analysis and why is it important in the spiral model?

    Answer: Risk analysis is the process of identifying potential problems that could affect the project and determining how to address them.

    Importance in spiral model: - Risk analysis is explicit in every iteration - Highest risks are addressed first - Helps decide whether to proceed with the project - Reduces overall project risk - Makes risk management a core activity

    Q15. Explain Fred Brooks' famous quotation about building a throwaway system.

    Answer: Quote: "The question is not whether to build a pilot system and throw it away. You will do that. The question is whether to plan in advance to build a throwaway."

    Meaning: - First attempts at building software are often learning experiences - The first version often needs to be discarded - Better to plan for this and build a throwaway prototype deliberately - This reduces risk and improves the final system - Supports the evolutionary approach

    Long Answer Questions¶

    Q16. Compare and contrast the Waterfall model and the Spiral model. What are the advantages and disadvantages of each? When would you use one over the other?

    Answer:

    Aspect Waterfall Spiral
    Approach Sequential Iterative with risk focus
    Risk handling Implicit, late Explicit, early
    Flexibility Low High
    Customer feedback Late Continuous
    Documentation Heavy Moderate
    Cost visibility Late Early

    Waterfall - Advantages: - Simple to understand - Clear milestones - Good for well-understood projects - Complete documentation

    Waterfall - Disadvantages: - Inflexible - Late problem discovery - No early working software - Poor for complex projects

    Spiral - Advantages: - Risk-driven - Early problem detection - Flexible - Good for large, complex projects

    Spiral - Disadvantages: - Complex to manage - Can be expensive - Requires risk analysis expertise - May not be suitable for small projects

    When to use: - Waterfall: Small, well-understood projects; regulatory requirements - Spiral: Large, complex, high-risk projects; innovative systems

    Q17. Explain the Phased-Release model. How does it address some of the problems of the waterfall model?

    Answer: Phased-Release Model: - System is divided into releases - Each release goes through complete waterfall phases - Releases are delivered sequentially - Later releases add more functionality

    Addressing waterfall problems: 1. Late delivery: Working software delivered after first release 2. No feedback: Customer sees and uses early releases 3. High risk: Risk reduced by delivering in parts 4. Changes difficult: Changes can be incorporated in later releases 5. All-or-nothing: If project fails, earlier releases still delivered

    Q18. Describe the Evolutionary model. What does it show that is not clear from the spiral model?

    Answer: Evolutionary Model: - System evolves through multiple versions - Each version is a complete, usable system - Versions progressively add functionality - Early versions may be prototypes

    What it shows that spiral doesn't: - The spiral model shows the process but not the system evolution - Evolutionary model explicitly shows how the system grows - Shows that early versions can be deployed - Emphasizes that the system is always in a deliverable state - Shows the progressive nature of development

    Q19. Discuss the Concurrent Engineering model. How does it account for the "divide and conquer" principle?

    Answer: Concurrent Engineering Model: - Multiple activities happen in parallel - Different teams work on different aspects simultaneously - Not strictly sequential like waterfall

    Divide and conquer: - System divided into subsystems - Different teams work on different subsystems concurrently - Integration happens when subsystems are complete - Reduces overall project time - Allows specialization

    Example: - UI team works on interface - Database team works on data layer - Business logic team works on core functionality - All happen at the same time

    Q20. What is eXtreme Programming (XP)? List and explain at least five of its key tenets.

    Answer: eXtreme Programming (XP) is an agile methodology that emphasizes customer satisfaction through rapid delivery of useful software.

    Key tenets:

    1. Incremental planning: Requirements recorded on story cards; frequent releases
    2. Small releases: Develop small, incremental releases to get rapid feedback
    3. Simple design: Do the simplest thing that could possibly work
    4. Test-first development: Write tests before writing code
    5. Refactoring: Continuously improve code structure
    6. Pair programming: Two programmers work together at one machine
    7. Collective ownership: Anyone can change any code
    8. Continuous integration: Integrate and build frequently
    9. On-site customer: Customer is part of the team
    10. Sustainable pace: Work at a pace that can be sustained indefinitely

    Q21. Compare the Agile approach with traditional process models. What are the advantages and drawbacks of agile methods?

    Answer:

    Aspect Traditional (Waterfall) Agile
    Process Heavy, prescriptive Light, adaptive
    Documentation Extensive Minimal, just enough
    Planning Upfront Continuous
    Changes Discouraged Embraced
    Customer involvement At milestones Continuous
    Team structure Hierarchical Self-organizing

    Agile - Advantages: - Responds to change quickly - Customer satisfaction through early delivery - Working software is primary measure of progress - Face-to-face communication - Technical excellence - Simplicity

    Agile - Drawbacks: - Can be difficult to scale to large projects - Requires experienced team members - Less documentation may cause problems later - Customer availability required - Hard to estimate costs upfront - May not suit all project types

    Q22. Explain how you would choose a process model for a particular project. What factors would you consider?

    Answer: Factors to consider:

    1. Requirements clarity:
    2. Well-understood → Waterfall

    3. Unclear → Prototyping, Spiral, Agile

    4. Project size:

    5. Small → Any model

    6. Large → Spiral, Phased-Release

    7. Risk level:

    8. Low → Waterfall, Incremental

    9. High → Spiral, Prototyping

    10. Customer availability:

    11. Available → Agile

    12. Limited → Waterfall

    13. Team experience:

    14. Experienced → Agile

    15. Novice → Waterfall with guidance

    16. Technology familiarity:

    17. Known → Waterfall

    18. New → Prototyping, Spiral

    19. Regulatory requirements:

    20. Strict documentation → Waterfall

    21. Flexible → Agile

    22. Time to market:

    23. Critical → Incremental, Agile
    24. Flexible → Waterfall

    5. FEASIBILITY STUDY - ANSWERS¶

    Short Answer Questions¶

    Q1. What is a feasibility study? What are its main objectives?

    Answer: A feasibility study is an analysis that determines whether a proposed project is viable and worth pursuing.

    Objectives: 1. Determine if the project is technically possible 2. Assess if the project is economically justified 3. Check if the project can be completed within constraints 4. Identify potential risks 5. Provide basis for go/no-go decision

    Q2. List the three main types of feasibility that should be considered.

    Answer: 1. Technical feasibility: Can it be built with available technology? 2. Economic feasibility: Is it cost-effective? Will benefits exceed costs? 3. Schedule feasibility: Can it be completed within the required timeframe?

    Q3. What is technical feasibility? Give an example of a project that might be technically infeasible.

    Answer: Technical feasibility assesses whether the proposed system can be developed with available technology, expertise, and resources.

    Example of technically infeasible project: - A real-time translation system in 1980s (before adequate speech recognition existed) - A system requiring more processing power than available - A system requiring integration with incompatible legacy systems without interfaces

    Q4. What is economic feasibility? How is it determined?

    Answer: Economic feasibility assesses whether the project is financially viable.

    Determined by: - Cost-benefit analysis - Calculating total cost of ownership (development + maintenance) - Estimating benefits (tangible and intangible) - Computing return on investment (ROI) - Break-even analysis

    If benefits exceed costs, the project is economically feasible.

    Q5. What is schedule feasibility?

    Answer: Schedule feasibility assesses whether the project can be completed within the required timeframe.

    Considerations: - Available resources (people, equipment) - Task dependencies - Critical path analysis - Risk factors that could cause delays - Market window (when product is needed)

    2. Technology assessment

    9. Schedule feasibility analysis:

    10. Timeline assessment
    11. Resource scheduling

    12. Critical path identification

    15. Will it solve the business problem?

    16. Legal/feasibility:

    17. Regulatory compliance
    18. Intellectual property issues

    Feasibility study report contents: - Executive summary - Project description - Analysis of each feasibility type - Risk assessment - Recommendations - Appendices with detailed calculations

    Q8. Discuss the importance of feasibility study in the software development process. What happens if a feasibility study is not conducted properly?

    Answer: Importance: - Prevents investment in unviable projects - Identifies risks early - Provides basis for decision making - Helps set realistic expectations - Can save significant costs - Ensures alignment with business goals

    Consequences of poor feasibility study: 1. Project failure: Discovering infeasibility after significant investment 2. Cost overruns: Underestimating costs 3. Schedule delays: Unrealistic timelines 4. Technical problems: Using inappropriate technology 5. Abandoned projects: Wasted resources 6. Damaged reputation: Failed projects affect organization credibility 7. Missed opportunities: Resources tied up in unviable projects
