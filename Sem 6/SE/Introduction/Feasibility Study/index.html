    Feasibility Study

    The Feasibility Study is the very first step in the software development process. Before a company spends millions of dollars and months of time, they need to make sure the project is actually a good idea.

    1. What is a Feasibility Study?¶

    The main goal of this study is to determine if a project is technically possible and financially worthwhile.

    Essentially, you are trying to answer three big questions:

    1. Can we build it? (Technical)
    2. Should we build it? (Economic)
    3. Can we finish it in time? (Schedule)

    The result of this stage is a Feasibility Report, which helps managers make a "Go/No-Go" decision.

    2. The Three Dimensions of Feasibility¶

    According to the sources, we look at feasibility through three lenses:

    I. Technical Feasibility¶

    We check if we have the "brainpower" and the "tools" to do the job.

    • Questions: Do we have the right programming skills? Is the technology (like a specific database or hardware) available?
    • Example: If a client wants an AI that can predict the future with 100% accuracy, the study would find this technically infeasible because that technology doesn't exist yet.

    II. Economic Feasibility (Cost-Benefit Analysis)¶

    This is all about the money. We compare the Costs of making the software against the Benefits it will bring.

    • Costs: Development price, setting up servers, training staff, and daily operations.
    • Benefits: Increased sales, saving time, or reducing errors.
    • The Rule: The benefits must be greater than the costs.

    III. Schedule Feasibility¶

    We check the clock. Can we finish the project by the customer's deadline?.

    • Example: If a company needs a tax-filing app ready by April, but the study shows it will take 8 months to build, the project is schedule infeasible.

    3. Activities During the Study¶

    To complete the study, a manager or analyst performs these steps:

    1. Understand the Problem: Get a rough idea of what the customer wants (what data goes in, what comes out).
    2. Propose Solutions: Think of 2 or 3 different ways to build the system.
    3. Compare Alternatives: Look at the cost and time for each different solution.
    4. Perform Cost-Benefit Analysis: Pick the best solution and prove it will save/make money.

    4. Case Study: The Mining Company (CFL)¶

    The sources provide an excellent example involving a mining company called CFL.

    • The Problem: CFL has over 50,000 workers. Mining is dangerous, and casualties are high. The current system for paying benefits (SPF) is too slow, and families are suffering while waiting for money.
    • The Feasibility Study Process:
      1. The manager visits the main office to see what the system needs to do.
      2. The manager visits the actual mine site to see how data is collected.
      3. The manager suggests a few different ways to build a faster software system.
      4. The manager presents the best solution to the officials.
    • The Decision: The officials look at the report and decide "Go" (start the project) or "No-Go" (cancel it).

    5. Cost Benefit Analysis¶

    • Identify the costs
      1. Development costs
      2. Setup cost
      3. Operational cost
    • Identify the benefits
      1. Quantifiable
      2. Non Quantifiable
    • Check whether benefit > cost

    6. Summary Table¶

    Feature Description
    When it happens At the very start of the life cycle.
    Main Document Feasibility Report.
    Economic Goal Benefits must outweigh the costs.
    Key Risk If you skip this, you might waste millions on a project that can't be finished.

    Note: In the sources, "Economic Feasibility" and "Cost-Benefit Analysis" are often used interchangeably because the financial "worth" is usually the deciding factor for businesses.
