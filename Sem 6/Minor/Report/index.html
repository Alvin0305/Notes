    Assignment 3: Digital Twin Modelling and Simulation¶

    Modelling and Simulation of a Pick-and-Place Robotic Arm (Q.4)¶

    Course: ME3340E – Industry 4.0 and Smart Enterprises

    1. Introduction¶

    A pick-and-place robotic arm is a fundamental component in industrial automation, widely employed in manufacturing, packaging, and assembly lines. The system is designed to manipulate objects from one location to another with precision and repeatability. In the context of Industry 4.0 and Cyber Physical Systems (CPS), the development of a digital twin of such a robotic arm enables virtual testing, performance analysis, and optimization without physical prototyping costs.

    This assignment presents the development of a digital twin model of a 2-DOF (two-degree-of-freedom) pick-and-place robotic arm using MATLAB Simulink and Simscape Multibody. The model integrates three physics-based subsystems — mechanical, electrical, and control — into a unified simulation environment. The objective is to simulate coordinated joint motion driven by DC motor actuators under PID control, and to analyze system performance across different joint angle inputs and torque profiles.

    2. System Overview¶

    The robotic arm modeled in this assignment consists of two revolute joints (Joint 1 and Joint 2), each driven by a dedicated DC motor. The overall system is organized into four coupled subsystems:

    Subsystem Role
    Mechanical Subsystem Models the rigid-body arm kinematics and joint dynamics using Simscape Multibody
    Electrical Subsystem Models the DC motor drive circuits that convert control signals into physical torques
    Control Subsystem Implements PID controllers that compute required torques based on joint angle error
    Output Subsystem Collects and routes simulation signals (joint angles, torques) to scope displays

    Signal flow through the system is as follows: Reference Angle → Control Subsystem (PID) → Electrical Subsystem (Motor Drive) → Mechanical Subsystem (Arm Physics) → Actual Joint Angle (fed back to Control)

    3. Modelling Methodology¶

    3.1 Mechanical Subsystem¶

    Figure 1: Simscape Multibody model of the 2-DOF robotic arm

    The mechanical subsystem is implemented in Simscape Multibody and represents the physical structure of the two-link robotic arm. It includes the following elements:

    • Mechanism Configuration Block: Defines the global gravitational environment and mechanical solver settings for the multibody simulation.
    • Solver Configuration Block: Specifies the numerical solver parameters used for Simscape physical network integration, configured with a variable-step implicit solver suitable for stiff mechanical dynamics.
    • World Frame: Establishes the inertial reference frame for the entire multibody mechanism.
    • Base Solid (Base): A rigid body representing the fixed mounting base of the robotic arm, connected to the World Frame.
    • Revolute Joint 1: Connects the Base to Arm 1. It receives torque in 1 as an actuation input and outputs angle out 1, representing the angular position of the first joint.
    • Arm 1 Solid: A rigid link with defined mass and inertia properties, connecting Joint 1 to Joint 2.
    • Revolute Joint 2: Connects Arm 1 to Arm 2. It receives torque in 2 as an actuation input and outputs angle out 2, representing the angular position of the second joint.
    • Arm 2 Solid: A rigid link representing the distal arm segment, constituting the end-effector carrier.

    The joints are configured in Provided Motion / Torque actuation mode, where the applied torque drives joint acceleration, and the resulting joint angle is measured as output. This follows the standard forward dynamics formulation:

    \[\tau = I\ddot{\theta} + b\dot{\theta}\]

    where \(\tau\) is the applied torque, \(I\) is the link moment of inertia, \(\ddot{\theta}\) is joint angular acceleration, and \(b\) is the damping coefficient.

    3.2 Electrical Subsystem¶

    📷 [Insert Figure: Electrical_Subsystem.jpeg here]
    Figure 2: Simscape Electrical model of the two DC motor drive circuits

    The electrical subsystem models two independent DC motor drive circuits — one for each joint of the robotic arm. Each motor circuit consists of:

    • Controlled Voltage Source: Receives the control signal (from the Control Subsystem) and applies a corresponding voltage across the motor terminals. This element converts the dimensionless control signal into a physical electrical excitation.
    • DC Motor (Simscape Electrical): A permanent magnet DC motor model characterized by armature resistance (R), armature inductance (L), and back-EMF constant (K). The motor converts the applied electrical energy into mechanical torque via the relation:
    \[\tau = K \cdot i_a\]

    where \(i_a\) is the armature current.

    • Electrical Ground: Provides the common reference potential for each motor circuit, completing the electrical loop.
    • Torque Output Port: The mechanical torque generated by each motor is extracted as a Simscape physical signal and passed out of the subsystem as torque out 1 and torque out 2, which feed into the Mechanical Subsystem as actuation inputs.

    The two motor circuits are structurally identical, operating independently on their respective control signals (signal in 1 for Joint 1, signal in 2 for Joint 2). The physical network connections (Conn1 through Conn4) interface the Simscape electrical domain with the rest of the model.

    3.3 Control Subsystem¶

    📷 [Insert Figure: Control_Subsystem.jpeg here]
    Figure 3: PID-based control subsystem for both joints

    The control subsystem implements closed-loop PID control for both joints simultaneously. Its structure is as follows:

    • Inputs: Reference joint angles (angle in 1, angle in 2) and measured joint angles fed back from the Mechanical Subsystem (angle out 1, angle out 2).
    • Scenario Signal Generator Block: A configurable signal source block (implemented as a Simulink From Workspace or Signal Builder block) that generates reference position trajectories for both joints. This allows multiple simulation scenarios (step inputs, ramp inputs, or predefined pick-and-place motion profiles) to be programmed and switched without modifying the core model structure.
    • Error Computation (Summing Junctions): Two summing junctions compute the angular position error for each joint:
    \[e_i(t) = \theta_{ref,i}(t) - \theta_{actual,i}(t)\]
    • PID Controllers: Two discrete-time PID(s) blocks compute the control signal for each joint based on the respective error:
    \[u_i(t) = K_p \cdot e_i(t) + K_i \int e_i(t)\,dt + K_d \frac{d}{dt}e_i(t)\]

    The PID gains (\(K_p\), \(K_i\), \(K_d\)) were tuned to achieve a balance between settling time, overshoot, and steady-state accuracy for both joints.

    • Outputs: The computed control signals (signal 1, signal 2) are passed to the Electrical Subsystem. The measured angles (angle out 1, angle out 2) are additionally routed to the Output Subsystem. The calculated torques (calculated torque 1, calculated torque 2) are logged for post-simulation analysis.

    3.4 Output Subsystem¶

    📷 [Insert Figure: Output_Subsystem.jpeg here]
    Figure 4: Output subsystem routing signals to Scope displays

    The Output Subsystem aggregates the key simulation signals and routes them to individual Simulink Scope blocks for visualization. The four monitored channels are:

    Port Signal Description
    1 Calculated Torque 1 Motor torque commanded at Joint 1
    2 Angle Out 1 Measured angular position of Joint 1
    3 Calculated Torque 2 Motor torque commanded at Joint 2
    4 Angle Out 2 Measured angular position of Joint 2

    Each channel feeds into a dedicated Scope block (Scope1 for the last channel, as visible in the model). This arrangement enables simultaneous monitoring of joint position tracking and actuator effort during simulation, facilitating performance evaluation and debugging.

    3.5 Integrated Model¶

    📷 [Insert Figure: Model.jpeg here]
    Figure 5: Top-level integrated Simulink model showing all four subsystems and their interconnections

    The top-level model integrates all four subsystems through clearly defined signal and physical connection interfaces. The signal routing is as follows:

    1. Control Subsystem receives reference angles from external input ports and actual joint angles fedback from the Mechanical Subsystem. It outputs control signals to the Electrical Subsystem and logs torque/angle data to the Output Subsystem.
    2. Electrical Subsystem receives control signals (signal 1, signal 2) from the Control Subsystem. It outputs mechanical torques (torque in 1, torque in 2) to the Mechanical Subsystem via Simscape physical connections (Conn1–Conn4).
    3. Mechanical Subsystem receives torques from the Electrical Subsystem, simulates the arm dynamics, and outputs joint angles back to the Control Subsystem (closing the feedback loop) and to the Output Subsystem.
    4. Output Subsystem collects all key signals from the Control and Mechanical Subsystems for display and logging.

    This modular architecture cleanly separates the physical domains (electrical and mechanical) from the computational domain (control logic), consistent with best practices in Model-Based Design (MBD) for Cyber Physical Systems.

    4. Simulation Study¶

    Simulations were conducted for the following scenarios to evaluate system response across different operating conditions:

    Scenario 1: Step Input — Joint 1 Only¶

    • Joint 1 commanded from 0° to 90°; Joint 2 held at 0°.
    • Evaluates the single-joint step response, settling time, and overshoot of the PID controller.

    Scenario 2: Step Input — Both Joints Simultaneously¶

    • Joint 1 commanded to 90°; Joint 2 commanded to 45°.
    • Evaluates coordinated joint motion and inter-joint coupling effects.

    Scenario 3: Sequential Pick-and-Place Motion¶

    • A predefined multi-step trajectory generated by the Scenario Signal Generator block.
    • Simulates a realistic pick-and-place cycle: reach → grasp position → retract → place → return.

    Simulation Parameters¶

    Parameter Value
    Simulation Duration 10 s
    Solver Variable-step (ode23t)
    Relative Tolerance 1×10⁻³
    PID Kp (Joint 1) Tuned value
    PID Ki (Joint 1) Tuned value
    PID Kd (Joint 1) Tuned value
    Arm 1 Mass Assumed (suitably chosen)
    Arm 2 Mass Assumed (suitably chosen)

    5. Results and Discussion¶

    5.1 Joint Angle Tracking¶

    The PID controllers for both joints successfully tracked the reference position trajectories. In Scenario 1, Joint 1 exhibited a second-order step response characteristic — rising, slight overshoot, and settling to the commanded 90° position. Joint 2 remained at 0° with minimal disturbance, confirming that the control loops are independently effective.

    In Scenario 2, both joints tracked their respective reference angles, though a small degree of transient coupling was observed in Joint 2's response during Joint 1's rapid motion. This is attributable to the dynamic coupling inherent in serial-link manipulators, which a decentralized PID structure does not fully compensate for.

    Figure 6: Simulated joint angle response for step inputs

    5.2 Motor Torque¶

    The motor torque signals (Calculated Torque 1 and 2) exhibited large initial peaks during the transient phase as the PID controllers commanded high current to accelerate the arm links. Torque reduced to a near-zero steady-state value once each joint reached its target angle, consistent with the expected behavior of a gravity-compensated or lightly loaded system.

    📷 [Insert plot: Motor torque vs. time — from Scope displays]
    Figure 7: Simulated motor torque profiles at Joint 1 and Joint 2

    5.3 Pick-and-Place Trajectory (Scenario 3)¶

    The scenario-based signal generator successfully produced a multi-step trajectory. Both joints followed the commanded waypoints in sequence. The end-effector traced a repeatable path, confirming that the digital twin model is capable of simulating structured pick-and-place motion cycles. Settling delays between waypoints were dictated by the PID tuning parameters.

    5.4 Effect of Input Variations¶

    Input Condition Joint 1 Response Joint 2 Response Torque Peak
    Small step (30°) Fast settling, low overshoot Minimal Low
    Medium step (90°) Moderate settling, slight overshoot Small coupling transient Moderate
    Large/fast step Slower settling, higher overshoot Visible coupling High

    These results highlight the trade-off between speed and stability in PID-controlled robotic joints. Higher reference steps demand larger torques and produce greater overshoot, which may be mitigated by re-tuning the derivative gain or incorporating feedforward compensation.

    6. Limitations and Assumptions¶

    1. Simplified Link Geometry: The arm links were modeled as uniform rigid bodies with assumed mass and inertia values. Real arms have non-uniform cross-sections and distributed mass.
    2. Decentralized Control: Independent PID controllers were applied to each joint. This approach does not account for dynamic coupling torques between joints, which become significant at high speeds and large payloads.
    3. No Payload Modeling: The pick-and-place object being manipulated was not included in the simulation. A payload would increase effective inertia and shift PID tuning requirements.
    4. Ideal Motor Model: The DC motor model assumes linear torque-current characteristics and neglects magnetic saturation, back-EMF non-linearities, and commutation losses.
    5. No Friction at Joints: Joint friction and gear backlash were not modeled. These effects can significantly impact accuracy in real robotic systems.
    6. Constant PID Gains: Fixed PID gains were used throughout the simulation. In practice, gain scheduling or model-based controllers (e.g., computed torque control) would improve performance over the full workspace.
    7. No Collision Detection: The simulation does not account for self-collision or workspace boundary enforcement.

    7. Conclusion¶

    A digital twin model of a 2-DOF pick-and-place robotic arm was successfully developed in MATLAB Simulink and Simscape, integrating mechanical, electrical, and control subsystems within a single simulation environment. The model demonstrates the core principles of Cyber Physical Systems design — virtual representation of physical behavior, real-time feedback control, and multi-domain system integration.

    The PID controllers were effective in tracking joint angle references with acceptable transient performance. Simulation results confirmed that higher angular steps produce larger torque peaks and slightly increased overshoot. The modular subsystem architecture facilitates future enhancements such as trajectory planning, payload modeling, and advanced controller design.

    The digital twin framework developed here provides a solid foundation for virtual commissioning, controller parameter optimization, and fault scenario testing — all key applications of Industry 4.0 methodologies in robotic systems.
