    • 1.1 Every point has an additive inverse
    • 1.2 Now let's understand why reflection is necessary
    • 1.3 What happens when we add a point to its inverse?

    These are exactly the questions worth asking before moving on to ECDH and ECDSA. In particular, the reflection across the x-axis isn't just a trick to obtain a different point. It is actually what makes the elliptic curve addition operation behave like ordinary addition in a mathematical group.

    Let's go one step at a time. I'll also work through all the numerical calculations using one consistent example, so you can verify every result.

    1. Why do we reflect the third intersection point?¶

    Let's start with the geometric construction.

    Suppose we have two points \(P\) and \(Q\) on an elliptic curve.

    1. Draw a line through \(P\) and \(Q\).

    2. It intersects the curve at a third point, which we'll call \(S\).

    3. Reflect \(S\) across the x-axis.

    4. The reflected point is defined as \(P+Q\).

    Elliptic curve method for discrete logarithms | Elliptic... | Fiveable

    fiveable.me

    But why?

    To understand this, we need to introduce a very important concept.

    1.1 Every point has an additive inverse¶

    In ordinary arithmetic, every number has an additive inverse.

    For example:

    \[ 5+(-5)=0 \]

    Similarly, in elliptic curve arithmetic, every point has an inverse.

    If:

    \[ P=(x,y) \]

    Then its inverse is:

    \[ -P=(x,-y) \]

    Why?

    Because the elliptic curve equation is:

    \[ y^2=x^3+ax+b \]

    Notice that replacing \(y\) with \(-y\) doesn't change anything:

    \[ (-y)^2=y^2 \]

    Therefore, if \((x,y)\) belongs to the curve, \((x,-y)\) also belongs to it.

    Geometrically, these two points are reflections of each other across the x-axis.

    Now comes the important part.

    We define the point at infinity, \(\mathcal O\), as the additive identity:

    \[ P+\mathcal O=P \]

    And the inverse operation satisfies:

    \[ \boxed{P+(-P)=\mathcal O} \]

    This is analogous to:

    \[ 5+(-5)=0 \]

    1.2 Now let's understand why reflection is necessary¶

    Suppose the line through \(P\) and \(Q\) intersects the curve at a third point \(S\).

    We define:

    \[ \boxed{P+Q=-S} \]

    Why this definition?

    Because it gives us the following useful relationship:

    \[ P+Q+S=\mathcal O \]

    In other words, the three intersection points of any straight line on the elliptic curve add up to the identity element.

    This is the geometric group law.

    If we simply took \(S\) as the result instead of \(-S\), we would not get this standard group law.

    The reflection is what connects the geometric intersection operation to the algebraic group structure.

    An important distinction: We could invent a different mathematical operation, but this particular definition gives us the associative addition operation, inverses, and identity that we need for a group.

    And ECC relies on this group structure.

    1.3 What happens when we add a point to its inverse?¶

    Consider:

    \[ P=(5,1) \]

    Its inverse is:

    \[ -P=(5,-1) \]

    Draw a line through these two points.

    It's a vertical line.

    The third intersection is the point at infinity.

    Therefore:

    \[ P+(-P)=\mathcal O \]

    This is the exceptional case where we don't get an ordinary finite coordinate pair.

    It is also why the point at infinity is necessary for elliptic curve arithmetic to form a complete group.

    2. Let's work with actual numbers¶

    We'll use one small elliptic curve throughout the remaining examples:

    \[ \boxed{y^2=x^3+2x+2\pmod {17}} \]

    Here:

    • \(a=2\)

    • \(b=2\)

    • \(p=17\)

    All coordinates are integers from 0 to 16.

    The curve equation means:

    \[ y^2\bmod17=(x^3+2x+2)\bmod17 \]

    We'll use these two points:

    \[ P=(5,1) \]
    \[ Q=(6,3) \]

    Let's verify that they actually belong to the curve.

    For \(P=(5,1)\):

    \[ LHS=1^2=1 \]
    \[ RHS=5^3+2(5)+2=137 \]
    \[ 137\bmod17=1 \]

    So \(P\) belongs to the curve.

    For \(Q=(6,3)\):

    \[ LHS=3^2=9 \]
    \[ RHS=6^3+2(6)+2=230 \]
    \[ 230\bmod17=9 \]

    So \(Q\) also belongs to the curve.

    2.1 Worked example: Adding two different points¶

    We want:

    \[ R=P+Q \]

    The formula for point addition is:

    \[ \lambda=\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}\pmod p \]
    \[ x_3=\lambda^2-x_1-x_2\pmod p \]
    \[ y_3=\lambda(x_1-x_3)-y_1\pmod p \]

    Step 1: Calculate the slope

    Substitute:

    \[ P=(5,1),\quad Q=(6,3) \]
    \[ \lambda=\frac{3-1}{6-5} \]
    \[ \boxed{\lambda=2} \]

    Step 2: Calculate the x-coordinate

    \[ x_3=2^2-5-6 \]
    \[ x_3=4-11=-7 \]

    Taking modulo 17:

    \[ \boxed{x_3=10} \]

    Step 3: Calculate the y-coordinate

    \[ y_3=2(5-10)-1 \]
    \[ y_3=-11 \]

    Taking modulo 17:

    \[ \boxed{y_3=6} \]

    Therefore:

    RESULT OF POINT ADDITION

    \[ \boxed{P+Q=(10,6)} \]

    Verified point on the curve

    Notice that the formula already gives us the reflected point.

    The geometric construction finds the third intersection and reflects it. The algebraic formula directly calculates the reflected point.

    We don't need to perform another reflection after calculating \(y_3\).

    2.2 Worked example: Doubling a point¶

    Now let's calculate:

    \[ 2P=P+P \]

    We use the same curve:

    \[ y^2=x^3+2x+2\pmod {17} \]

    The doubling formula is:

    \[ \lambda=\frac{3x_1^2+a}{2y_1}\pmod p \]
    \[ x_3=\lambda^2-2x_1\pmod p \]
    \[ y_3=\lambda(x_1-x_3)-y_1\pmod p \]

    Our point is:

    \[ P=(5,1) \]

    Step 1: Calculate the tangent slope

    \[ \lambda=\frac{3(5)^2+2}{2(1)} \]
    \[ \lambda=\frac{77}{2}\pmod {17} \]

    Now, modular arithmetic doesn't allow ordinary division.

    Instead, we multiply by the modular inverse.

    The modular inverse of 2 modulo 17 is 9:

    \[ 2\times9=18\equiv1\pmod {17} \]

    Therefore:

    \[ \lambda=77\times9\pmod {17} \]

    First:

    \[ 77\bmod17=9 \]

    Then:

    \[ 9\times9=81 \]
    \[ \boxed{\lambda=13} \]

    Step 2: Calculate the x-coordinate

    \[ x_3=13^2-2(5) \]
    \[ x_3=169-10=159 \]
    \[ 159\bmod17=6 \]

    Therefore:

    \[ \boxed{x_3=6} \]

    Step 3: Calculate the y-coordinate

    \[ y_3=13(5-6)-1 \]
    \[ y_3=-14 \]
    \[ -14\bmod17=3 \]

    Therefore:

    \[ \boxed{y_3=3} \]

    Our final answer:

    RESULT OF POINT DOUBLING

    \[ \boxed{2P=(6,3)} \]

    Verified point on the curve

    Interestingly, \(2P\) is exactly the point \(Q\) we used in the previous example!

    So for our particular curve:

    \[ \boxed{2(5,1)=(6,3)} \]

    3. Worked example: Calculating \(10G\)¶

    Now let's put everything together.

    This is particularly important because scalar multiplication is the operation actually used in ECC.

    We'll continue using exactly the same curve:

    \[ E: y^2=x^3+2x+2\pmod {17} \]

    Let's choose:

    \[ \boxed{G=(5,1)} \]

    For now, think of \(G\) as our generator point. We'll discuss what makes a point a generator in the next section.

    We want to calculate:

    \[ \boxed{10G} \]

    3.1 Why not just add G ten times?¶

    We could do:

    \[ 10G=G+G+G+G+G+G+G+G+G+G \]

    But that would require nine point additions.

    Instead, we'll use the Double-and-Add algorithm.

    First, represent 10 in binary:

    \[ 10=1010_2 \]

    Or:

    \[ 10=8+2 \]

    Therefore:

    \[ \boxed{10G=8G+2G} \]

    We only need to calculate \(2G\), \(4G\), \(8G\), and then add \(2G\) to \(8G\).

    3.2 Calculate 2G¶

    We already did this!

    \[ G=(5,1) \]
    \[ \boxed{2G=(6,3)} \]

    3.3 Calculate 4G¶

    Now double \(2G\).

    \[ 4G=2(2G) \]

    Our current point is:

    \[ P=(6,3) \]

    Calculate the slope:

    \[ \lambda=\frac{3(6)^2+2}{2(3)} \]
    \[ =\frac{110}{6}\pmod {17} \]

    The modular inverse of 6 modulo 17 is 3:

    \[ 6\times3=18\equiv1\pmod {17} \]

    Therefore:

    \[ \lambda=110\times3\bmod17 \]
    \[ \lambda=8\times3\bmod17 \]
    \[ \boxed{\lambda=7} \]

    Calculate the coordinates:

    \[ x_3=7^2-2(6) \]
    \[ x_3=49-12=37\equiv3\pmod {17} \]
    \[ y_3=7(6-3)-3 \]
    \[ y_3=18\equiv1\pmod {17} \]

    Therefore:

    \[ \boxed{4G=(3,1)} \]

    3.4 Calculate 8G¶

    Double \(4G\):

    \[ 8G=2(4G) \]

    Our current point is:

    \[ P=(3,1) \]

    Calculate the slope:

    \[ \lambda=\frac{3(3)^2+2}{2(1)} \]
    \[ =\frac{29}{2}\pmod {17} \]

    The modular inverse of 2 is 9.

    \[ \lambda=29\times9\bmod17 \]
    \[ \lambda=12\times9\bmod17 \]
    \[ \boxed{\lambda=6} \]

    Calculate the coordinates:

    \[ x_3=6^2-2(3)=30\equiv13\pmod {17} \]
    \[ y_3=6(3-13)-1=-61\equiv7\pmod {17} \]

    Therefore:

    \[ \boxed{8G=(13,7)} \]

    3.5 Calculate 10G = 8G + 2G¶

    Now we have:

    \[ 8G=(13,7) \]
    \[ 2G=(6,3) \]

    We need to add these two different points.

    Calculate the slope:

    \[ \lambda=\frac{3-7}{6-13} \]
    \[ =\frac{-4}{-7}=\frac47\pmod {17} \]

    The modular inverse of 7 is 5:

    \[ 7\times5=35\equiv1\pmod {17} \]

    Therefore:

    \[ \lambda=4\times5=20\equiv3\pmod {17} \]

    Calculate the x-coordinate:

    \[ x_3=3^2-13-6 \]
    \[ x_3=9-19=-10\equiv7\pmod {17} \]

    Calculate the y-coordinate:

    \[ y_3=3(13-7)-7 \]
    \[ y_3=18-7=11 \]

    Therefore:

    FINAL RESULT

    10G = (7, 11)¶

    For the curve y² = x³ + 2x + 2 (mod 17), with G = (5, 1).

    3.6 Summary of our calculations¶

    Here's the complete sequence:

    Operation Result
    \(G\) (5, 1)
    \(2G\) (6, 3)
    \(4G\) (3, 1)
    \(8G\) (13, 7)
    \(10G=8G+2G\) (7, 11)

    Notice that we used point doubling three times and one point addition.

    That's the beauty of Double-and-Add!

    For a scalar with hundreds of bits, we need only a few hundred point operations, rather than an astronomically large number of individual additions.

    4. What exactly is special about the generator point G?¶

    This is an important question because \(G\) is not just any arbitrary point.

    Let's understand its role.

    4.1 What does a generator actually generate?¶

    Suppose we have a point \(G\).

    We repeatedly add it to itself:

    \[ G,\ 2G,\ 3G,\ 4G,\ 5G,\ldots \]

    Eventually, we'll reach the point at infinity:

    \[ nG=\mathcal O \]

    At that point, the sequence starts repeating.

    The smallest positive integer \(n\) satisfying:

    \[ \boxed{nG=\mathcal O} \]

    is called the order of the point G.

    The points generated by repeatedly adding \(G\) form a cyclic subgroup.

    If this subgroup contains every point in the elliptic curve group, then \(G\) is a generator of the entire group.

    Otherwise, it is a generator of a subgroup.

    This distinction is important in cryptography.

    4.2 Let's examine our example¶

    Remember our curve:

    \[ y^2=x^3+2x+2\pmod {17} \]

    And:

    \[ G=(5,1) \]

    If we keep calculating multiples of \(G\), we eventually get:

    Scalar Point
    \(1G\) (5, 1)
    \(2G\) (6, 3)
    \(3G\) (10, 6)
    \(4G\) (3, 1)
    \(5G\) (9, 16)
    \(6G\) (16, 13)
    \(7G\) (0, 6)
    \(8G\) (13, 7)
    \(9G\) (7, 6)
    \(10G\) (7, 11)

    If we continue, eventually:

    \[ 19G=\mathcal O \]

    And 19 is the smallest positive integer for which this happens.

    Therefore:

    \[ \boxed{\operatorname{order}(G)=19} \]

    Since 19 is prime, all the non-infinity points generated by \(G\) form a subgroup of 19 elements.

    In fact, this particular curve has exactly 19 points including \(\mathcal O\), so \(G\) generates the entire group.

    4.3 Does G have to satisfy any particular properties?¶

    Yes!

    In real cryptographic implementations, the generator point is selected with very specific properties.

    Some important requirements are:

    1. G must lie on the elliptic curve.

    It must satisfy:

    \[ y_G^2\equiv x_G^3+ax_G+b\pmod p \]

    2. G must have a sufficiently large order.

    We want the subgroup generated by \(G\) to be extremely large.

    Otherwise, an attacker could simply try all possible scalar values.

    3. Its order should be a large prime number.

    Let:

    \[ nG=\mathcal O \]

    Ideally, \(n\) is a very large prime.

    This makes the discrete logarithm problem in the subgroup difficult.

    4. The curve itself must be non-singular.

    For the curve:

    \[ y^2=x^3+ax+b \]

    we require:

    \[ \boxed{4a^3+27b^2\not\equiv0\pmod p} \]

    This prevents degenerate curves with singularities.

    4.4 Is G randomly selected?¶

    Usually, no.

    In standardized cryptographic curves, the curve parameters and generator point are fixed and publicly specified.

    For example, a curve specification defines:

    • Prime modulus \(p\).

    • Curve coefficients \(a,b\).

    • Generator coordinates \(G_x,G_y\).

    • Order \(n\) of the generator.

    • Cofactor \(h\).

    Everyone using that curve uses the same public generator.

    The private key, however, is randomly generated.

    For example:

    \[ d=123456789 \]

    The corresponding public key is:

    \[ Q=dG \]

    So:

    • \(G\) is public and fixed by the curve parameters.

    • \(d\) is randomly generated and private.

    • \(Q\) is calculated from \(d\) and \(G\), and is public.

    One more detail: the total number of points on a curve does not always equal the order of the generator. Sometimes the group contains a larger number of points, and \(G\) generates only a subgroup of prime order \(n\). The cofactor is:

    \[ h=\frac{|E(\mathbb F_p)|}{n} \]

    This is why selecting a suitable generator and handling subgroup membership correctly are important parts of ECC implementation.

    5. Connecting everything to the actual ECC key-generation process¶

    Now you should be able to understand this entire process:

    ECC key generation

    Public curve parameters

    \(p,\ a,\ b,\ G,\ n\)

    Generate private key

    Choose random integer:

    \[ 1\leq d<n \]

    Scalar multiplication

    Calculate public key

    \[ Q=dG \]

    Private

    \(d\)

    Never disclose

    Public

    \(Q\)

    Can be shared

    The security comes from the difficulty of reversing:

    \[ Q=dG \]

    Even though point addition and doubling are efficient, finding \(d\) from \(G\) and \(Q\) is computationally difficult for properly selected cryptographic parameters.

    One important caveat: Our example uses a prime modulus of only 17. It is purely for understanding the mathematics and offers absolutely no cryptographic security.

    What I recommend learning next¶

    You now have the foundation for ECC. I'd suggest learning these topics in this order:

    1. Modular arithmetic: Especially modular inverses and why division is replaced by multiplication by an inverse.

    2. Elliptic Curve Group: Identity, inverse, closure and associativity.

    3. Scalar multiplication: Double-and-Add algorithm in detail.

    4. ECDLP: Why reversing scalar multiplication is difficult.

    5. ECDH: How two parties establish a shared secret.

    6. ECDSA: How elliptic curves are used for digital signatures.

    One particularly useful next exercise would be implementing point addition, point doubling and scalar multiplication in C++. That would make the mathematics much more concrete.
